## Lesson 8 — Spoken Patterns

### Lesson objectives

- Recognize full and contracted affirmative forms of `to be`.
- Use `I'm`, `you're`, `he's`, `she's`, `it's`, `we're`, and `they're` naturally.
- Form negatives with `I'm not`, `isn't`, `aren't`, and the alternative `’s/’re not` patterns.
- Complete the special question tag after `I am`: `I'm ..., aren't I?`.
- Ask and answer simple `to be` questions with standard short answers.
- Recognize `ain't` as informal spoken English and map it to a standard equivalent without using it as the default grammatical form.

### Grammar focus and short explanation

In natural spoken English, forms of `to be` often join the subject: `I am → I'm`, `she is → she's`, and `they are → they're`. Negative forms can contract in two common ways: `she isn't / she's not` and `they aren't / they're not`. With `I am not`, the standard negative statement is `I'm not`. In the fixed standard question tag after `I'm`, this lesson teaches `aren't I?`: `I'm late, aren't I?` It does not accept `amn't I?`, and it does not treat `ain't I?` as the standard answer.

`Ain't` appears only at recognition level under the visible label **Informal English** or **Informal Spoken English**. Every such activity supplies a standard equivalent after completion: `I ain't tired → I'm not tired`, `He ain't here → He isn't here`, `They ain't ready → They aren't ready`, and `We ain't late → We aren't late`.

### Vocabulary and reviewed content

Productive zoo vocabulary: zoo, animal/animals, lion, zebra, giraffe/giraffes, guide, tree, strong, beautiful, afraid, ready, and happy. Recognition-only vocabulary in the reading: explore, entrance, beside, nature, and group. Familiar school language remains in selected examples: student, teacher, friend, class, school, and classroom. The lesson also reviews pronouns, personal information, affirmative and negative `to be`, questions, short answers, register, listening, and shadowing.

### Target structures and question patterns

- `I am Daniel. → I'm Daniel.`
- `She is from Mexico. → She's from Mexico.`
- `I am not a teacher. → I'm not a teacher.`
- `He is not twelve. → He isn't twelve. / He's not twelve.`
- `They are not at home. → They aren't at home. / They're not at home.`
- `He is the guide. → He's the guide.`
- `Is Emily near a giraffe? — Yes, she is.`
- `Lucas is not afraid. → Lucas isn't afraid.`
- `Are they at the zoo? — Yes, they are.`
- `Are you ready? — Yes, I am. / No, I'm not.`
- `Is he sad? — No, he isn't.`
- `I'm right, aren't I?`
- `I'm late, aren't I?`
- `I'm your friend, aren't I?`
- `I'm in the right classroom, aren't I?`

### Reading and complete dialogues

**Reading — At the Zoo**

> Today is Tuesday. Ben, Anna, Lucas, and Emily are at the zoo. They are not late. They're ready to explore. The guide says, “Good morning, everyone. Are you ready?” Ben says, “Yes, I am. I'm ready.” Anna says, “I'm ready too. I'm not tired today.” Lucas is happy. He isn't afraid. He is with his friends.
>
> Emily is near the giraffes. She isn't at the entrance. She is beside a tall tree. The guide points to a lion and says, “He's strong.” Then she points to a zebra and says, “It's beautiful too.” After that, the guide looks at the group and says, “You're all ready. We're ready to see more animals.” The students smile. They're happy because they can enjoy nature and understand contractions in English.

> Guide: Is he afraid?
> Anna: No, he isn't.
> Guide: Is he happy?
> Anna: Yes, he is.

**Dialogue 18 — They Ain't Late**
**Informal Spoken English**

> Lucas: No, they ain't.
> Guide: Are they near the giraffes?
> Lucas: Yes, they are.

Standard equivalent: `No, they aren't. They aren't late, and they're ready.`

### Practice and Writing

The app separates affirmative contractions, negative contractions, questions and short answers, the fixed tag `I'm ..., aren't I?`, natural reading/listening/dialogue work, and informal `ain't` recognition. Zoo vocabulary is distributed through the existing grammar practice while familiar school language remains in selected items. Equivalent standard full and contracted forms are accepted when the prompt allows either. A contraction-specific prompt requires the contraction. Standard-English prompts never accept `ain't`; exact informal listening transcription accepts the exact `ain't` form heard.

### Speaking and Review

Speaking and shadowing exercises use `audioValue`, so the existing interface provides normal playback, slower replay, microphone capture, comparison, correction, and completion feedback. Shadowing targets include affirmative and negative contractions, questions, `aren't I?`, natural dialogue, and recognition-level informal speech.

### Seven-day editorial plan

1. Day 1: 15 affirmative-contraction exercises.
2. Day 2: 15 negative-contraction exercises.
3. Day 3: 15 question-and-short-answer exercises.
4. Day 4: 10 exercises on the fixed standard tag `I'm ..., aren't I?`.
5. Day 5: 15 natural reading, listening, dialogue, and shadowing exercises in the zoo context.
6. Day 6: 10 visible informal-`ain't` recognition, register, meaning, transcription, dialogue, and shadowing exercises.
7. Day 7: a generated 20-item mixed Final Test with 8 listening-writing, 6 shadowing, and 6 spoken-answer items.

**New content:** affirmative contractions, negative contractions, questions and short answers, `I'm ..., aren't I?`, recognition-level informal `ain't`, and A1 zoo vocabulary in a balanced familiar context.
**Reviewed:** personal information, greetings, pronouns, age, country, days, dates, and affirmative `to be`.  
**Difficulty increase:** recognition → controlled production → listening discrimination → register classification → mixed assessment.
**Preparation for Lesson 9:** natural contractions, confirmation, and correction in real-life dialogues.  
**Pronunciation/listening:** contrast `is/isn't`, `are/aren't`, `I'm/aren't I?`, and informal `ain't` against its standard equivalents. No external audio file is required; `audioValue` supplies reproducible TTS text.

## Lesson 9 — Practical Speaking

### Lesson objectives

- Select natural responses in common A1 interactions.
- Complete, organize, listen to, and produce structured dialogues.
- Introduce oneself and another person; ask about name, age, country, dates, spelling, and numbers.
- Confirm and correct information using contractions and negative `to be`.

### Grammar focus and short explanation

Practical speaking uses familiar grammar as connected turns. A question and its answer must match: `Where are you from? — I'm from Brazil`, not an unrelated age or name response. Confirmation uses `Are you…? / Is she…?`; correction uses `No, … isn't/aren't` plus the accurate information.

### Vocabulary, structures, and questions

Greetings, introductions, classmate/friend/sister, classroom requests, day, date, birthday, spelling, phone number, house/phone-number exchange, `Nice to meet you`, `Could you spell your name?`, `I don't understand`, and `Could you repeat, please?`.

### Complete dialogues

**Dialogue 1 — Meeting someone**

> Sofia: Hi, I'm Sofia. What's your name?  
> Ben: I'm Ben. Nice to meet you.  
> Sofia: Nice to meet you too. Where are you from?  
> Ben: I'm from Brazil.

**Dialogue 2 — Classroom information**

> Teacher: Could you spell your name?  
> Lucas: L-U-C-A-S.  
> Teacher: Are you eleven?  
> Lucas: No, I'm not. I'm twelve.

**Dialogue 3 — Confirming information**

> Maya: Is she your sister?  
> Ben: No, she isn't. She's my friend.  
> Maya: What's her phone number?  
> Ben: It's 555-0182.

### Practice, Writing, Speaking, and Review

The trails recognize 15 natural responses, complete 15 conversational patterns, reverse-match responses to opening lines, check the three dialogues, and produce 15 guided spoken interactions. Review asks students to introduce themselves, ask the day and birthday, request spelling, correct a relationship, and exchange a phone number.

### Reading and Checking — A New Classmate

> On Tuesday, Sofia meets Ben at Learnendo School. “Hi, I’m Sofia,” she says. Ben introduces himself and asks where she is from. Sofia is from Chile, and she is twelve. Ben is not twelve; he is eleven. Sofia spells her last name, R-O-J-A-S, for the class list. Then Ben introduces his friend Maya. Maya is from Mexico, and her birthday is on August ninth. Sofia first thinks Maya is Ben’s sister. Ben corrects her: “No, she isn’t. She’s my friend.” Before class, the students compare phone numbers and confirm the date. It is September third. Their teacher arrives at nine o’clock, and everyone is ready.

The 14 checking questions cover time, place, countries, ages, spelling, friendship, birthday, correction, numbers, date, teacher arrival, and readiness.

### Seven-day editorial plan

1. Recognize appropriate responses in 15 real-life exchanges.
2. Complete core conversational structures in writing.
3. Match answers back to their correct opening questions.
4. Listen to and analyze three complete dialogues.
5. Produce 15 guided lines and mini-exchanges.
6. Read a coherent interaction and answer 14 questions.
7. Consolidate introductions, dates, spelling, correction, and numbers.

**New content:** dialogue management, response appropriateness, spelling/number exchange, and correction as connected interaction.  
**Reviewed:** greetings, name, age, country, days, dates, birthdays, numbers, affirmative/negative `to be`, and contractions.  
**Difficulty increase:** single response → completion → reverse matching → dialogue → personal mini-dialogue.  
**Preparation for Lesson 10:** asking when, using dates, birthdays, and scheduled class times.

## Lesson 10 — Months & Seasons

### Lesson objectives

- Use `in` with months, years, seasons, and parts of the day.
- Use `on` with days and dates.
- Use `at` with clock times, noon, midnight, and night.
- Identify the four seasons and discuss simple calendar events.
- State that the lesson’s month/season groupings use Northern Hemisphere examples.

### Grammar focus and short explanation

Use `in` for a broad period (`in January`, `in summer`, `in 2026`), `on` for a day/date (`on Monday`, `on April eighth`), and `at` for an exact point (`at seven o'clock`, `at noon`, `at night`). The season examples follow the Northern Hemisphere; seasons occur in different months in the Southern Hemisphere.

### Vocabulary and structures

Spring, summer, fall/autumn, winter, calendar, picnic, trip, festival, noon, midnight, night; all months, dates, ordinals, years, birthdays, and `when` questions.

### Complete dialogues

**Dialogue 1 — A birthday**

> Maya: When is your birthday?  
> Ben: It's on January fifteenth.  
> Maya: Is it in winter?  
> Ben: In this Northern Hemisphere example, yes, it is.

**Dialogue 2 — A class trip**

> Teacher: When is our trip?  
> Leo: It's on April eighth.  
> Teacher: What time?  
> Leo: At seven o'clock.  
> Maya: That's in spring in our Northern Hemisphere calendar.

### Practice, Writing, Speaking, and Review

The app contains 15 contrasting time expressions, 15 `in/on/at` completions, 15 question-answer pairs, dialogue checks, and 15 oral calendar statements/questions. Review combines month, date, clock time, birthday, class schedule, contractions, negatives, and a hemisphere-qualified season statement.

### Reading and Checking — Our 2026 Calendar

> The Learnendo class makes a calendar for 2026. The examples use seasons in the Northern Hemisphere. Spring is in March, April, and May. The class picnic is on April eighth at noon. Summer is in June, July, and August, and Maya’s birthday is on July twelfth. Fall, or autumn, is in September, October, and November. A school festival is on October second at seven o’clock. Winter is in December, January, and February. Ben likes to read at night in winter. The students know that seasons occur in different months in the Southern Hemisphere, so these month groups are not universal. Their calendar shows months, dates, seasons, and exact times clearly.

The 14 checking questions cover year, hemisphere, all four season groups, picnic, birthday, festival, reading time, Southern Hemisphere qualification, and calendar contents.

### Seven-day editorial plan

1. Recognize months, seasons, dates, and times with their prepositions.
2. Complete 15 contrasts with `in/on/at`.
3. Answer calendar and schedule questions, including correction of wrong prepositions.
4. Use two dialogues about birthday and travel.
5. Produce personal calendar statements and questions.
6. Read an annual calendar and answer 14 questions.
7. Consolidate prepositions, date/time, contractions, and hemisphere-aware seasons.

**New content:** four seasons and basic `in/on/at` time expressions.  
**Reviewed:** days, months, dates, ordinals, birthdays, numbers, `to be`, `when`, contractions, and negatives.  
**Difficulty increase:** lexical association → contrast → schedule reasoning → personal calendar production.  
**Preparation for Lesson 11:** varied `when`, `what date`, and calendar questions.

## Lesson 11 — Asking Questions

### Lesson objectives

- Choose `what`, `who`, `where`, `when`, and `how old` by meaning.
- Build WH-questions with the correct order: question expression + `to be` + subject/complement.
- Match questions and answers and correct word order.
- Ask and answer personal, calendar, place, person, and age questions.

### Grammar focus and short explanation

The question word tells the information needed: `who` asks about a person, `where` about a place, `when` about time, `what` about information/day/month/date, and `how old` about age. With `to be`, place the verb before the subject: `Where are you from?`, not `Where you are from?`.

### Target questions

`What is your name?`; `Who is he/she?`; `Where are you from?`; `Where is the teacher?`; `When is your birthday?`; `What day is it today?`; `What month is it?`; `What is the date?`; `How old are you/is she?`; plus class, season, and location variations already represented in the trails.

### Complete dialogues

**Dialogue 1 — A new student**

> Maya: What's your name?  
> Amir: My name is Amir.  
> Maya: Where are you from?  
> Amir: I'm from Egypt.  
> Maya: How old are you?  
> Amir: I'm eleven.

**Dialogue 2 — People, place, and time**

> Amir: Who is she?  
> Leo: She's Ms. Green.  
> Amir: Where is she?  
> Leo: She's in the classroom.  
> Amir: When is English class?  
> Leo: It's on Monday at nine.

### Practice, Writing, Speaking, and Review

Students choose the question expression, reorder 15 questions, match 15 questions/answers, analyze the two dialogues, and orally ask/answer all target patterns. The final review corrects order and integrates contractions, personal information, dates, classroom location, seasons, and time prepositions.

### Reading and Checking — Questions for Amir

> Ms. Green welcomes a new student to the Monday class. His name is Amir, and he is eleven. Maya asks, “Where are you from?” Amir says he is from Egypt. Leo asks, “When is your birthday?” Amir’s birthday is on December second. The students look at the class calendar. It is September third, and their next school event is on October tenth at noon. Amir points to Ms. Green and asks, “Who is she?” Maya answers, “She’s our English teacher.” Then Amir asks where his book is. It is on his desk. At the end, Ms. Green asks, “What questions can you ask now?” The students use what, who, where, when, and how old to learn about their new classmate.

The 14 checking questions cover person, name, age, country, birthday, day/date/event, teacher, object location, speaker, and meanings of `who/how old`.

### Seven-day editorial plan

1. Recognize question expressions in 15 contexts.
2. Reorder and write 15 complete questions.
3. Match 15 WH-questions to complete answers.
4. Use two dialogues to distinguish person/place/time/personal information.
5. Ask and answer all patterns orally.
6. Read about a new student and answer 14 questions.
7. Consolidate selection, order, answers, dates, places, and contractions.

**New content:** WH-question selection and order with `to be`.  
**Reviewed:** personal information, calendar, numbers, ordinals, seasons, `in/on/at`, contractions, negatives, and dialogues.  
**Difficulty increase:** word recognition → sentence construction → question generation and personal production.  
**Preparation for Lesson 12:** asking about a past school day through a small number of supportive `did` questions, without a full simple-past unit.

## Lesson 12 — Past Tense Regular Verbs

### Lesson objectives

- Recognize and write common regular past forms ending in `-ed`.
- Hear and produce the three principal pronunciations `/t/`, `/d/`, and `/ɪd/`.
- Classify the ending by the final sound of the base verb, not only by its last written letter.
- Use short past statements about yesterday, class, a birthday, or a school day.

### Grammar and pronunciation focus

The lesson introduces regular past forms, not the whole simple past. In these examples, written `-ed` has three sounds. After applicable voiceless sounds it is `/t/` (`worked`, `talked`, `asked`, `helped`, `watched`); after vowels and applicable voiced sounds it is `/d/` (`played`, `called`, `opened`, `learned`, `cleaned`, `listened`); after `/t/` or `/d/` it adds a syllable `/ɪd/` (`wanted`, `needed`, `started`, `visited`).

### Vocabulary and target structures

Worked, talked, asked, helped, watched, played, called, opened, learned, cleaned, listened, wanted, needed, started, visited; yesterday, last Monday, class, question, number game, calendar, library, story.

### Complete dialogues

**Dialogue 1 — Yesterday in class**

> Teacher: What did you do yesterday?  
> Ben: I played a number game and helped Leo.  
> Teacher: Did class start at nine?  
> Ben: Yes. It started at nine.

**Dialogue 2 — After class**

> Maya: I visited the library yesterday.  
> Leo: What did you do there?  
> Maya: I looked at books and listened to a story.  
> Leo: I called my friend after class.

The `did` questions support these two contexts only; the lesson does not expand into a full irregular-verb or simple-past explanation.

### Practice, Writing, Speaking, and Review

Students recognize 15 past forms, write the same 15 base-to-past transformations, classify 15 spoken endings, check the dialogues, and say 15 contextual sentences grouped by sound. The review revisits all sound groups, form writing, and a short guided account of yesterday.

### Reading and Checking — Yesterday at Learnendo

> Yesterday was Monday, September third. The Learnendo students started class at nine o’clock. Ms. Green opened the lesson and asked a question about birthdays. Maya answered first and spelled her name. Ben wanted to practice numbers, so the class played a short number game. Leo talked about the seasons and helped a new student with the calendar. At noon, everyone visited the school library. They looked at books, listened to a short story, and learned new words. After class, Maya called her friend and talked about the good day. The students practiced many regular past verbs. They noticed that the written ending was always -ed in these examples, but its sound was /t/, /d/, or /ɪd/.

The 14 checking questions cover day/date/time, teacher, birthday question, students, game, calendar help, library, story, phone call, written ending, and all three sounds.

### Seven-day editorial plan

1. Recognize 15 regular past forms in familiar vocabulary.
2. Write base verb → past form.
3. Listen to and classify `/t/`, `/d/`, `/ɪd/` using the final sound rule.
4. Use two short dialogues about yesterday.
5. Produce 15 contextual sentences with focused pronunciation.
6. Read a coherent school-day account and answer 14 questions.
7. Consolidate form, sound, listening, speaking, and a guided account.

**New content:** regular `-ed` past forms and their three pronunciations.  
**Reviewed:** days, dates, numbers, spelling, classroom vocabulary, questions, greetings, `to be` as support, and spoken patterns.  
**Difficulty increase:** form recognition → spelling → auditory classification → contextual production.  
**Preparation beyond Workbook 1:** later systematic study of past statements and questions; irregular verbs are intentionally not developed here.  
**Audio note:** all pronunciation targets are text supplied to the existing browser/API TTS pipeline. No external recorded-audio asset is declared or pending in this implementation.
